Unit 1

Volume measures how much space a solid object fills.

This topic will build cubic units, count unit cubes, use length x width x height, and split composite solids into parts.

5.MD.3

Understand Volume

See volume as space filled by same-size unit cubes.

5.MD.4

Count Cubic Units

Build layers of cubes and count them efficiently.

5.MD.5

Use Formulas

Connect counting cubes to length x width x height and additive volume.

1. Cubes 2. Rows & layers 3. Base x height 4. Expressions 5. Missing side 6. Split solids

Idea 1 (Unit 1)

Volume counts cubic units, not just flat squares.

A face can show square units, but a solid has depth. To measure volume, every hidden and visible unit cube has to be counted.

Idea 2 (Unit 1)

Rows, columns, and layers build a rectangular prism.

Change the dimensions to see one layer form first. Then stack identical layers to fill the prism.

Idea 3 (Unit 1)

Base area times height means repeated layers.

First count the cubes in one base layer. The height tells how many equal layers are stacked.

Idea 4 (Unit 1)

Different expressions can describe the same prism.

A product can name dimensions, one layer repeated, or a different grouping of the same cubes. The amount stays fixed.

Idea 5 (Unit 1)

A missing dimension is a missing factor.

When the volume is known, divide by the part you can see: one layer, one side length, or a base area.

Idea 6 (Unit 1)

Composed solids can be split into rectangular prisms.

Split the shape into prisms that do not overlap, find each volume, then add the parts back together.

Unit 8 Teach Me

Use volume to model real objects before calculating.

Unit 8 revisits volume through estimation contexts: cube collections, large wagons, sand bags, roof area, and rainfall.

Idea 1 (Unit 8)

Volume is a modeling choice before it is a formula.

Unit 8 uses sugar cubes, wagons, sand bags, and rainfall. The habit is to choose the dimensions and units first, then multiply length x width x height.

Unit 2 - Section A

Fractions can be the answer to a fair sharing problem.

Start with a real story, split each whole into equal shares, then connect the picture to a division expression, a fraction, and a mixed number when the share is more than one whole.

1. Sharing 2. Two names 3. Top and bottom 4. More than one 5. Try a problem

Idea 1 (Unit 2)

Equal sharing creates fraction pieces.

If whole sandwiches are shared fairly, each whole is cut into the same number of equal pieces. One person's share is made from one piece of every sandwich.

Idea 2 (Unit 2)

The division and the fraction are two names for one amount.

The story, picture, division expression, and fraction are not separate facts. They are four views of the same fair share.

Idea 3 (Unit 2)

The top tells how many. The bottom tells what kind.

The numerator counts how many fraction pieces are in one share. The denominator names the size of each piece by telling how many equal pieces make one whole.

Idea 4 (Unit 2)

Sometimes one fair share is more than one whole.

When there are more sandwiches than people, each person can receive whole sandwiches plus a fraction. That is why improper fractions and mixed numbers describe the same amount.

Idea 5 (Unit 2)

Try your own sharing problem.

Choose how many sandwiches and how many people. The app will show the fair share as a picture, division expression, fraction, and mixed number if it needs one.

Unit 2 - Section A Practice

Practice fair sharing, division-as-fraction, and mixed numbers.

Each problem asks for an answer, then connects the answer back to the same kind of picture used in Teach Me. Hints reveal the thinking without giving everything away at once.

Idea 1 (Unit 2)

A unit fraction of a number starts by splitting the number into equal groups.

To find one part, divide the whole number by the denominator. That one group is the unit fraction of the whole number.

Idea 2 (Unit 2)

A non-unit fraction takes more than one equal group.

First find one denominator-sized group. Then the numerator tells how many of those equal groups to take.

Idea 3 (Unit 2)

Divide first, then multiply by the numerator.

The diagram explains the expression. The denominator splits the whole number, and the numerator counts how many equal groups are used.

Idea 4 (Unit 2)

One diagram can match many expressions.

The curriculum asks students to match diagrams and expressions because the same shaded groups can be described in several correct ways.

Idea 5 (Unit 2)

A rectangle can show a whole number times a fraction.

Area still means length times width. When one side is fractional, the unit squares are split into equal fractional rows.

Idea 6 (Unit 2)

A fractional side length can be greater than one.

A fraction like 5/3 is one whole plus 2/3 more. The area is larger than the whole-number length because the fractional side is taller than 1.

Idea 7 (Unit 2)

Mixed-number area can be split into whole area plus fractional area.

Decomposing the mixed number shows the distributive property without naming a rule first: multiply the whole part, multiply the fraction part, then combine.

Idea 8 (Units 2 and 6)

Estimate before calculating.

The fraction factor tells whether the product should be smaller than, the same as, or larger than the whole-number factor.

Idea 9 (Unit 3)

A fraction of a fraction is the overlap of two choices.

Shade one fraction in one direction. Then take another fraction of that shaded part in the other direction. The overlap is the product.

Idea 10 (Unit 3)

The rule comes from counting the picture.

The denominators make the grid. The numerators count the rows and columns that overlap. Click each part to connect the symbols to the model.

Idea 11 (Unit 6)

Multiplication can resize an amount.

In Unit 6, students compare products before multiplying. A scale factor less than 1 shrinks the amount, 1 keeps it the same, and a factor greater than 1 grows it.

Idea 12 (Unit 6)

A scale factor moves the product on a number line.

The factor tells where the product lands compared with the starting amount: before it, on it, or beyond it.

Idea 13 (Unit 6)

Compare products before calculating exactly.

In Unit 6, students learn to decide whether a product must shrink, stay the same, or grow before doing any exact multiplication.

Idea 1 (Unit 8)

In fraction product games, numerator spots and denominator spots do different jobs.

Unit 8 game boards ask students to place spun numbers before multiplying. A large numerator grows a fraction; a large denominator shrinks it.

Unit 3 - Section B

Dividing fractions asks how many groups fit, or how big each share is.

Tape diagrams keep the two division meanings separate: split a known amount into equal shares, or count how many small groups fit inside a whole amount.

1. Two stories 2. Split a small part 3. Count small parts 4. Order and check

Idea 1 (Unit 3)

The same word "divide" can mean two different actions.

Sharing division asks how big each share is. Measurement division asks how many groups of a known size fit.

Idea 2 (Unit 3)

A unit fraction divided by a whole number splits one small part.

If 1/3 is shared by 4 people, the starting amount is already smaller than one whole. Each share must be smaller than 1/3.

Idea 3 (Unit 3)

A whole number divided by a unit fraction counts how many small pieces fit.

If each serving is 1/3 of a whole, then every whole contains 3 servings. More wholes means more servings to count.

Idea 4 (Unit 3)

Order matters, and multiplication checks the division.

1/3 ÷ 4 and 4 ÷ 1/3 use the same numbers, but the tape diagram action is different. The quotient times the divisor should return the dividend.

Unit 3 - Section B Practice

Practice splitting unit fractions and counting unit-fraction groups.

These cards keep tape and strip diagrams visible so students decide whether the story is asking for share size or how many groups fit.

Unit 4

Multi-digit multiplication and division are place-value machines.

This topic will connect area models, partial products, standard multiplication, and division strategies based on place value.

5.NBT.5

Multiply Multi-Digit Numbers

Move from area models to the standard algorithm.

5.NBT.6

Divide Multi-Digit Numbers

Assignments

Practice Sequence

Matches the January multiplication and division practice.

Idea 1 (Unit 4)

Multiplication starts as area split by place value.

A multi-digit product is easier to trust when each rectangle has a meaning: tens by tens, tens by ones, ones by tens, and ones by ones.

Idea 2 (Unit 4)

The standard algorithm is compressed partial products.

The written algorithm gets shorter, but the place values are still there. A row that starts under the tens place is a row of tens.

Idea 3 (Unit 4)

Where a digit sits can matter more than the digit itself.

Unit 4 digit games are really place-value games. A digit in the tens place does ten times the work of the same digit in the ones place.

Idea 4 (Unit 4)

Division can remove useful chunks instead of guessing one big answer.

Partial quotients keep division flexible: choose a useful multiple of the divisor, subtract it, and keep the leftovers visible.

Idea 5 (Unit 4)

Different partial-quotient paths can still land at the same quotient.

One student may subtract a big chunk first. Another may use more smaller chunks. The check is whether the total quotient and remainder make sense.

Idea 6 (Unit 4)

A remainder is not finished until the story says what it means.

The same leftover can mean extra, round up, or share more precisely. Unit 4 asks students to interpret the leftover, not just write "R."

Idea 7 (Unit 4)

Application problems start by choosing the missing piece.

Area and volume stories use multiplication and division together. Decide what is missing first, then choose the operation.

Unit 6

Add and subtract fractions by making the pieces match first.

Compare, add, and subtract fractions by renaming them into same-size pieces. Mixed numbers and improper fractions come from packing full sets into wholes.

Unit 8 Teach Me

Use fraction game boards to make strategic sums and differences.

Unit 8 turns fraction addition and subtraction into games where students choose placements before calculating.

1. Compare 2. Add/Subtract 3. Make wholes 4. Regroup 5. Story problems 6. Strategies 7. Mixed numbers 8. Game strategy

Idea 1 (Unit 6)

Compare fractions after the pieces are the same size.

Piece counts are only fair to compare when the pieces have the same denominator. Rename first, then compare the shaded pieces.

Idea 2 (Unit 6)

Add or subtract fractions by using same-size pieces.

When denominators match, pieces can be joined or removed directly. When they do not match, rename first so every piece has the same size.

Idea 3 (Unit 6)

Enough fraction pieces can pack into wholes.

If the numerator reaches the denominator, the shaded pieces fill one whole. Extra pieces become the leftover fraction part.

Idea 4 (Unit 6)

Mixed-number subtraction may need one whole to open into fraction pieces.

If there are not enough fraction pieces to subtract, rename one whole as denominator-sized pieces. The amount stays the same, but now the pieces can be removed.

Idea 5 (Unit 6)

Story problems still need same-size pieces.

First name the whole. Then rename the fractions so the story pieces can be added or subtracted fairly.

Idea 6 (Unit 6)

Different strategies can explain the same fraction operation.

A common denominator gives exact pieces. Benchmarks and number lines help check whether the answer is reasonable.

Idea 7 (Unit 6)

Mixed-number operations track wholes and fraction pieces.

Keep the whole-number part and the fraction part visible. Rename or regroup only when the fraction pieces need to match.

Idea 1 (Unit 8)

Fraction games reward thinking about piece size before adding or subtracting.

In Greatest Sum and Greatest Difference, every spun digit has a job. Put larger digits where they make the amount bigger, and smaller digits where they control the denominator.

Unit 5 Teach Me

Decimals extend place value into tenths, hundredths, and thousandths.

Unit 5 starts with equal parts of one whole, then uses grids, number lines, fraction names, expanded form, and helper zeros to compare and round decimals.

1. Fill places 2. Empty whole places 3. Thousandths 4. Decimal zeros 5. Same amount 6. Place values 7. Compare & round

Idea 1 (Unit 5)

Ones can pack into tens, and tens can open into ones.

Ten loose cookies become one plate of 10. One plate of 10 can also open back into 10 loose cookies when subtraction needs more ones.

Idea 2 (Unit 5)

Zeros on the left are empty bigger places.

Hundreds and tens are cookie groups. A hundreds plate holds 100 cookies, a tens plate holds 10 cookies, and ones are single loose cookies.

Idea 3 (Unit 5)

Thousandths are hundredths split into ten smaller equal pieces.

Unit 5 grids make the whole stay fixed. First split one whole into hundredths. Then split each hundredth into 10 thinner pieces, so the whole has 1,000 equal parts.

1 whole = 10 tenths = 100 hundredths = 1,000 thousandths
Tenths
0.1 = 1/10

One tenth is one of 10 equal parts.

Hundredths
0.01 = 1/100

Each tenth can split into 10 hundredths.

Thousandths
0.001 = 1/1000

Each hundredth can split into 10 thousandths.

The whole does not get bigger. The pieces get smaller, so it takes more of them to cover the same whole.

Idea 4 (Unit 5)

Zeros on the right rename decimal pieces.

One tenth of a pizza can be one big slice or ten smaller hundredth pieces. The shaded pizza area does not change.

Idea 5 (Unit 5)

Decimal, fraction, and picture are three views of one amount.

The written form can change while the visual amount stays fixed. That is the habit we want before comparing or calculating.

Idea 6 (Unit 5)

A digit's value depends on its decimal place.

The same digit can mean ones, tenths, hundredths, or thousandths. Moving one place left makes a value 10 times as much; moving one place right makes it one tenth as much.

0.4 is 10 times 0.04 and 100 times 0.004

thousandths 4 0.004

Compare decimals from left to right because larger places decide first.

Idea 7 (Unit 5)

Number lines make comparing, ordering, and rounding visible.

A decimal belongs in one interval. Helper zeros can show matching places, but the number line shows the size of the amount.

0.72 is between 0.7 and 0.8
0.7 0.75 0.8 0.72
Compare 0.72 > 0.7

0.72 is to the right of 0.70.

Order 0.7, 0.72, 0.8

Left to right gives least to greatest.

Round 0.72 rounds to 0.7

It is closer to 0.7 than 0.8.

Decimal operations work when each place lines up with the same kind of piece.

Add, subtract, multiply, and divide decimals by thinking about tenths, hundredths, and thousandths as fraction pieces with matching names.

Idea 1 (Unit 5)

Before comparing, adding, or subtracting, make the places match.

Helper zeros work like common denominators. They let each tenths, hundredths, or thousandths place line up with the same kind of piece.

Decimal regrouping is the same 10-for-1 trade in smaller pieces.

Ten hundredths make one tenth. Ten thousandths make one hundredth. Addition and subtraction work when those trades stay visible.

Units 5 and 6

Expressions and powers of ten are compact ways to describe math actions.

This topic will connect order of operations, parentheses and brackets, simple written expressions, metric conversion, and powers of ten.

1. Decimal neighbors 2. Place values 3. Powers of 10 4. Digit movement 5. Metric conversion 6. Predict first 7. Customary units 8. Multi-step 9. Table

Idea 1 (Unit 5)

Decimal place values are a chain of times 10 and one tenth.

Unit 5 prepares powers of ten by making neighboring decimal places visible. A place-value chart is a set of relationships, not just a row of labels.

1 tenth = 10 x 1 hundredth = 100 x 1 thousandth

Going back left reverses the arrows: each place is 10 times the value of the place to its right.

Idea 1 (Unit 6)

Neighboring place values are connected by 10.

A digit does not have one fixed value. Its place tells what it is worth: moving one place left makes it 10 times as much, and moving one place right makes it one-tenth as much.

Idea 2 (Unit 6)

A power of ten tells how many times 10 is used as a factor.

Exponents are compact notation. Instead of writing 10 x 10 x 10, we can write 103. The exponent counts the 10s.

Idea 3 (Unit 6)

The digits move to places with different values.

The decimal point marks the ones place. When we multiply or divide by powers of 10, the digits shift into larger-value or smaller-value places.

Idea 4 (Unit 6)

Metric conversions rename the same measurement.

Meters, centimeters, millimeters, and kilometers are different unit names for length. The amount stays the same, but the number changes because the unit size changes.

Idea 5 (Unit 6)

Before calculating, predict whether the number should get larger or smaller.

Conversions make more sense when the unit size is named first. Smaller units need more counts; larger units need fewer counts.

Idea 6 (Unit 6)

Customary conversions use relationship cards, not powers of 10.

Inches, feet, yards, cups, pints, quarts, and gallons use different conversion factors. The same idea still works: rename the same amount.

Idea 7 (Unit 6)

Multi-step measurement problems work one rename at a time.

Complex conversion problems become manageable when each arrow names the unit relationship and the operation.

Idea 8 (Unit 6)

A conversion table keeps the amount fixed while the unit changes.

Tables help students notice whether the count grows or shrinks as the unit gets smaller or larger.

Unit 7

Coordinates, shape attributes, and patterns all help us describe math precisely.

Unit 7 starts with exact locations on a grid, then uses exact attributes to classify shapes, and finally turns matching pattern values into points.

1. Exact location 2. Ordered pairs 3. Vertices 4. Attributes 5. Hierarchy 6. Always? 7. Rules to points 8. Context graphs

Idea 1 (Unit 7)

A coordinate grid makes a location exact.

The Unit 7 materials begin with drawing and describing shapes. A plain description can be misunderstood, but numbered grid lines let two people land on the same point.

Plain words "Put the corner near the middle."

Close, but different students might choose different places.

Grid lines "Use this row and this column."

Better, but we still need names for the lines.

Coordinates "Put the point at (4, 6)."

The numbers tell exactly how far right and how far up.

Point "Land on the intersection."

A coordinate names where two grid lines meet, not the inside of a box.

Coordinates are a language for locations. The point is where the two named grid lines meet.

Idea 2 (Unit 7)

An ordered pair is a trip from the origin: right first, then up.

In an ordered pair like (4, 6), the first number gives the horizontal position and the second number gives the vertical position.

Idea 3 (Unit 7)

Coordinates can describe a whole shape, not just one point.

A shape on a coordinate grid is a set of vertices. Naming the vertices lets another person draw the same shape in the same place.

Idea 4 (Unit 7)

Shape names come from attributes.

Students sort shapes by properties such as parallel sides, equal side lengths, right angles, and angle size. Rotation does not change the attributes.

Idea 5 (Unit 7)

Some shape families live inside bigger shape families.

Unit 7 uses the inclusive definition of trapezoid: a trapezoid has at least one pair of opposite sides parallel. That means parallelograms count as trapezoids.

Idea 6 (Unit 7)

Always, sometimes, and never need examples and counterexamples.

Shape statements are not guesses. A single counterexample can prove a statement is not always true.

Idea 7 (Unit 7)

Two rules can make ordered pairs.

Generate two patterns, match the terms in the same position, then graph those matches. A point tells two values at the same time.

Idea 8 (Unit 7)

A graph point can describe a real situation.

The same coordinate idea works when the axes are not just "rule 1" and "rule 2." A point can mean coins and cents, length and perimeter, or length and area.
